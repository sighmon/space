Method and sources

What is drawn

Four schematics. The mesh is a low Earth orbit ring. The Moon view places Earth and the Moon side by side and draws an elliptical lunar frozen orbit, of the family ESA’s Moonlight programme uses for its south-pole communications satellite, plus a small halo just past the Moon for an Earth–Moon L2 relay. The Mars view puts Earth and Mars on circular heliocentric orbits and a relay on a tight loop around Mars. The conjunction view freezes Mars on the far side of the Sun and puts one relay 60° ahead of Mars, the Sun–Mars L4 region. Orbits move faster than real time. Pulses move on the light-time scale named in the dock.

Light time is path length divided by 299,792.458 km/s. The lunar mean distance used for the straight Earth–Moon hop is 384,400 km. One astronomical unit is 149,597,870.7 km. Mars’s orbit is drawn at 1.524 AU. The conjunction path via L4 is the sum of the two straight legs, Earth to the L4 point and that point to Mars. The far-side path is the same kind of sum: 64,500 km from the far side to a halo 64,500 km beyond the Moon, then 448,900 km from that halo back to Earth.

A rate is printed only when the source states one. The mesh rate is 100 Gbps, from Travis Brashears, SPIE, 2024, including the longest reported link of 5,400 km. The Mars cruise rate is the Deep Space Optical Communications span, 8.3 to 267 Mb/s, measured from 0.36 to 2.68 AU with a 22 cm flight terminal. It is not interpolated to the angle on screen. The conjunction rate, 28 to 44 Mb/s return, is a Jet Propulsion Laboratory study of a proposed 50 cm optical terminal in a long-period Sun–Mars L4 or L5 orbit, not a flight. No Starlink rate is published at lunar or Martian range, so those hops show light time and say so. No link budget was calculated.
